Model comparison

We compared seven machine learning algorithms: Logistic Regression, Gaussian Naive Bayes, K-Nearest Neighbours, Random Forest, LightGBM, XGBoost and a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP). The top three models were XGBoost, LightGBM and Random Forest, with cross-validation F1 scores of 0.9396, 0.9392 and 0.9385. These results were very close, especially considering that the variation between folds was approximately 0.010. We ultimately selected LightGBM because it achieved the best test Top-1 accuracy of approximately 90.05%, had the joint-best log loss of 0.089, and trained around four times faster than Random Forest.

Error analysis

We also analysed the mistakes made by LightGBM to understand where the model struggled. Among its incorrect predictions, 42% involved selecting the reverse-direction twin of the correct road, and 79% involved roads sharing a node with the correct road, usually around intersections. Only a small proportion of errors (4%) occurred because the correct road was missing from the candidate list. This suggests that most errors were caused by ambiguity between nearby roads rather than candidate generation itself.

What did we learn from each model?

  1. Logistic Regression provided a simple baseline, but it struggled to capture complex relationships between features. Its performance suggests underfitting, meaning the model was not complex enough to learn the patterns in the data. Its most influential feature was proj_overshoot, with a weight of -5.0.
  2. Gaussian Naive Bayes performed the worst among the compared models. One possible reason was that several features were correlated, causing the model to effectively double-count some information. This affected its predictions, resulting in relatively low precision of 0.53.
  3. K-Nearest Neighbours achieved a reasonable F1 score, but its inference was relatively slow, taking around 8 seconds. It also produced less informative probability estimates.
  4. Random Forest performed well and achieved an F1 score of 0.933. However, training was relatively slow, taking approximately 93 seconds.
  5. LightGBM gave us the best overall results. Although there was some mild overfitting, the model generalised well to unseen trips. This made it our final classifier.
  6. XGBoost performed almost identically to LightGBM. Both models achieved the same log loss of approximately 0.089, making them very competitive alternatives.
  7. Multi-Layer Perceptron also achieved good results, but in some folds training stopped after reaching the maximum limit of 40 epochs.

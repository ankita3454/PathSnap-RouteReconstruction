Final evaluation. For the evaluator dataset, we used the final frozen system. Importantly, we did not retrain or retune the model after examining these trips. All the results were obtained directly from the API logs (Fig 19) and the results/evaluator/evaluator_results.csv file.

Overall performance. Across 19,674 GPS points, the classifier accuracy was 90.79%, and the accuracy after applying the HMM was 93.86%. The mean route F1 was 0.962 and the mean processing time was 0.70 seconds. The HMM improved the results on all 10 evaluator trips. The final performance was also consistent with the student-test results, where the HMM achieved 92.51% accuracy. This suggests that the system generalises well to unseen trips.

Best-performing trip. Our best result was obtained on Trip 00000087. The accuracy improved from 96.07% to 99.71% after applying the HMM. This shows how effectively the transition model can improve route predictions when the road network provides enough information to resolve ambiguity.

Weakest-performing trip: Trip 00000015 (accuracy 88.28%, route F1 0.779). We investigated this trip to understand why its performance was lower. One important observation was that this trip involved walking rather than driving. The median speed was approximately 1.5 m/s when measured over 10-second windows, but it appeared to be 7.2 m/s over one-second windows. This happened because GPS jitter was sometimes larger than the actual movement of the person. As a result, the estimated heading became unreliable. We also found that reverse-direction road twins accounted for 80% of the additional predicted route length. Another contributing factor was the difference between the training and evaluator data: most of our training trips involved vehicles, with a median speed of approximately 42.5 km/h. Trip 15 involved walking, creating a distribution shift that the model was not specifically trained to handle.

Possible improvement. One possible solution would be to calculate heading over a fixed travel distance rather than a fixed time interval. This could make heading estimates more reliable when the movement is slow. However, we did not apply this change because it would introduce information leakage into the evaluation process. If we exclude Trip 15, the overall evaluator accuracy increases to 96.84%.

Another interesting case: Trip 17. Trip 17 had a continuity score of 87.6%, but its route F1 was still very high at 0.976. This happened because the road network was particularly dense. In such networks, the strict continuity metric can make the result appear worse than it actually is. Despite the lower continuity score, the reconstructed route contained zero actual breaks.
